1. The short version

Your medical procedure records are encrypted on your device, with a key we do not have and cannot obtain. We cannot read your radiation history. We cannot list it, count it, or describe it. Not with database access, not with server access, not under legal compulsion, and not at your own request.

MySievert is a personal record-keeping tool. You log medical procedures that involved radiation; the app attaches published reference dose values and keeps a running history for you. It does not assess those values, and it does not tell you what they mean. Everything about those procedures is encrypted before it ever leaves your device.

What we actually hold on our servers, for each account, is:

  • An opaque identifier from your sign-in provider
  • Two encrypted byte arrays we cannot decrypt or parse
  • One boolean recording whether you hold a paid entitlement

That is the complete list. There is no procedure table, no record count, no activity log, no analytics database, and no advertising identifier anywhere in this product.

We do not sell your personal data. We do not share it for advertising. We serve no ads. We run no analytics SDK. We never request location permission, for any reason, ever.

This policy applies to the MySievert mobile applications, the MySievert API operated by Apex, and any MySievert web client we may publish. It does not apply to your healthcare providers, your imaging facility, your insurer, or any third-party service you separately choose to use.

3. How the system is built, because the privacy claims depend on it

Most privacy policies describe a promise. This one describes an architecture, because the promise is a consequence of the architecture and not the other way round.

A. Your device holds the data and the key

The local store on your phone is the source of truth for your history. The app works fully offline, including logging. Server synchronisation is backup, never a dependency.

A 256-bit data encryption key (the “DEK”) is generated on your device, by your device, at the moment you log your first record — not when you create an account. An account with no records has no key material anywhere.

B. The server holds ciphertext and does not have the key

Every record — the exposure type, the date, the dose values, the associated catalog entry, any note you write, and the derived totals — is serialised into a single document, padded, encrypted on your device, and transmitted to us as an opaque byte array.

The server stores that array. It has no schema for its contents, no index over it, and no code path that parses it. We hold a second opaque array containing wrapped copies of your key, each wrapped under a key we do not possess.

C. Authentication and encryption are separate systems

Signing in proves who you are. It does not unlock anything. Possessing a valid credential for your account grants access to your ciphertext, and nothing more. Your sign-in provider never holds and cannot derive your encryption key.

The cost of this design, stated plainly. There is no key escrow, no administrative override, no support escalation, and no recovery path that does not run through your own device or your recovery code. If you lose both your device and your recovery code, your history is permanently unreadable — by you and by us. We accept that cost deliberately, because it is the direct source of every protection described in this policy. See Section 8.

4. Information we collect

A. Account identifier

You sign in through Firebase Authentication, operated by Google. We receive and store a single value: an opaque user identifier (a Firebase uid). Our servers handle no name, no email address, and no device identifier in connection with your account.

Your chosen sign-in provider — for example, a Google account — will itself process the identity information required to authenticate you, under that provider’s own privacy policy. Where the provider makes an email address or display name available to the app on your device, the app uses it locally to show you which account you are signed into. That information is not stored in the MySievert account record on our servers.

B. Your encrypted vault

One opaque byte array containing your entire encrypted history. We cannot decrypt it and we cannot parse it.

C. Your encrypted key wraps

One opaque byte array containing wrapped copies of your data encryption key. It is held as a single undifferentiated blob rather than a structured list, deliberately, so that we cannot even determine which kinds of wraps your account holds — whether you have set up a recovery code, for instance.

D. Entitlement

One boolean recording whether your account holds a paid entitlement, and, where a purchase has been made, a one-way hash of the store purchase token. The hash exists solely to prevent one purchase from entitling many accounts. The raw token is never stored.

E. Technical metadata we necessarily hold

A policy claiming “nothing” would be untrue and would undermine everything else in this document. The following is the complete list of what our storage layer necessarily reveals, and what we do about each item:

F. Aggregate usage statistics

We maintain anonymous aggregate counts of which procedures are most commonly logged across the platform, so we know which entries in our reference catalog deserve verification effort and refinement. These counts are population totals. They are not, and by construction cannot be, associated with any individual account.

That claim does not rest on a schema. It rests on four constraints, each of which is enforced rather than merely intended, and each of which is load-bearing:

  1. No IP address is retained anywhere in the chain. The client address is stripped from the connection by the first component in our request pipeline, before any other handler, logger, or metric can observe it. Forwarded-address headers are removed at the same point. Automated tests fail our build if any component that would record a client address is introduced. Nothing in this application rate-limits, partitions, geolocates, or shards by client address, because there is no client address to use.
  2. Usage counts carry no authentication token. A count sent with a credential attached is an identified count, whatever its payload says. Usage reporting is unauthenticated by design, so no server-side moment exists in which a counter and an account identity are present in the same request.
  3. One counter per report, never a set. A report containing “three of procedure X and two of procedure Y” is a fingerprint that describes a person. Counts are reported singly.
  4. Batched, with jitter. Arrival time is a timestamp whether or not anyone stores it. Counts do not leave your device at the moment you log a record.

If a future change would make any one of these four constraints untrue, this policy changes in the same release, or the change does not ship.

G. Support correspondence

If you email us, we hold your message and your email address so we can reply. Please do not include details of your medical procedures in a support email — we do not need them, we cannot use them to help you, and an email is not protected by the encryption described above.

5. Information we do not collect

  • Your location. The app never requests location permission — not coarse, not precise, not once. This is a permanent product rule, not a current default. We will not build a feature that logs a procedure at the facility you are standing in.
  • Your IP address. Removed from the connection before anything downstream can observe it, and excluded from web server request logging.
  • Analytics or behavioural telemetry. No analytics SDK is present in the application. No screen views, no session recording, no funnels, no event streams.
  • Crash reports containing anything clinical. Crash reporting is off by default. If it is ever enabled, it will carry no screen names, view names, record identifiers, catalog identifiers, or search terms. Automated build checks fail on any clinical term appearing in a type, member, or route name.
  • Advertising identifiers. The app contains no advertising SDK and serves no advertisements.
  • Contacts, photos, calendar, microphone, camera, or SMS.
  • Data from Apple Health, Google Health Connect, or any other health platform. The app neither reads from nor writes to them.
  • Data from your providers, insurers, imaging facilities, or health information exchanges. Everything in your history is there because you typed it.
  • Biometric identifiers. Where you unlock the app or a platform key store with a fingerprint or face, that check happens entirely on your device, through the operating system. We never receive biometric data.

6. How we use what we hold

The only permitted uses of the information in Section 4 are:

  • Authenticating you and associating your session with your encrypted blobs
  • Storing and returning your ciphertext so your history survives a lost or replaced device
  • Detecting and rejecting conflicting concurrent writes so a sync cannot lose data
  • Determining whether your account holds a paid entitlement
  • Preventing abuse of the service, using per-account limits only
  • Improving the accuracy and coverage of our public reference catalog, using the aggregate counts described in Section 4(F)
  • Responding to you when you contact us

We cannot use your health records for any purpose whatsoever, including our own, because we cannot read them. We do not profile you, score you, or evaluate your records — we could not do so even if we wanted to.

7. Encryption standard

Stated specifically, so that it can be evaluated rather than taken on faith. These parameters are implemented, verified against byte-for-byte conformance vectors in continuous integration on every client platform, and amended in the same commit as any change to the cryptography.

PropertyValue
Content encryptionAES-256-GCM (NIST SP 800-38D)
Key length256 bits
Nonce96 bits, from a cryptographically secure RNG, unique per encryption
Authentication tag128 bits
Additional authenticated dataThe envelope header (version, key id, nonce)
Key derivationHKDF-SHA256 (RFC 5869), extract-then-expand
Key wrappingAES-256-GCM
Reference catalog signingEd25519, public key pinned in each app binary
Recovery code128 bits of system-generated entropy, with a checksum
TransportTLS 1.2 or higher

Implementations use platform-native cryptographic providers only — no bundled third-party crypto library. Negative conformance vectors covering tampered tags, tampered ciphertext, rewritten key identifiers, version downgrade, and wrong keys must all be rejected for a build to pass.

Because payloads are already ciphertext before transmission, TLS is a second layer rather than the only one: a transport failure would expose ciphertext, not records. Data at rest on our servers is ciphertext in a database that has never seen the key. Data at rest on your device is additionally protected by platform facilities — encrypted local storage keyed through the Android Keystore or the iOS Keychain with file protection.

Key custody

  • The DEK is generated on your device, at first record.
  • The DEK never leaves your device in unwrapped form. It is never transmitted to, stored on, or derivable by any server operated by Apex or by any processor.
  • Wrapped copies are held server-side and are opaque to us: they are ciphertexts of your key under keys we do not have.
  • Wraps are produced independently by your platform key store (Android Keystore via Block Store, or the iOS Keychain) and by your recovery code.
  • Where a platform’s backup service cannot provide end-to-end encryption for the wrapped key, the app will not enable cloud backup of it, and a recovery code becomes mandatory instead. Your key does not reach a platform vendor unprotected.

Apex Technology Group, Inc. cannot decrypt a user’s health records. There is no key escrow, no administrative override, and no recovery path that does not run through the user’s own device or their recovery code.

8. Recovery, and irreversible loss

Read this before you rely on the app to keep your history.

Your records are recoverable to a new device in two ways: through your platform key store, if the platform successfully migrates it, or through your recovery code. If neither is available, your history cannot be recovered by anyone, including us. There is no exception, no escalation, and no support ticket that changes this.

Please set up your recovery code when the app offers it, store it somewhere you will still have it in two years, and export a PDF copy of your history periodically. A recovery code is a passphrase; treat it as one. Anyone who obtains both your recovery code and access to your account can read your history.

We detect the state in which your account holds server-side data that your device cannot open, and we tell you so directly rather than showing you an empty history. We also give you practical routes to rebuild it — a prior PDF export, your own paperwork, or a list of studies with dates from your imaging facility.

11. Retention and deletion

We retain your encrypted blobs, account identifier, and entitlement boolean for as long as your account exists. There is nothing else to retain.

You can delete your account from within the app, or by emailing us. Deletion removes your account record, your encrypted vault, and your encrypted key wraps from our production systems. Because we hold no derived data, no analytics rows, and no activity table, there is no second copy of your information anywhere for us to miss.

Deletion propagates to our processors within thirty (30) days, including expiry from routine encrypted backups. Aggregate counts described in Section 4(F) are not deleted, because they are population totals that were never associated with you and cannot be traced back to you.

Deletion is permanent. Once deleted, your history cannot be restored by us under any circumstances.

Deleting the app from your device does not delete your account. Deleting your account does not cancel a store subscription; manage that through Google Play or the App Store.

13. HIPAA does not apply, and what that means

Apex Technology Group, Inc. is not a covered entity and, in operating MySievert, is not a business associate. HIPAA does not govern this app, and we do not claim that it does. Information you enter here is not protected health information under HIPAA merely because it is medical in nature.

We say this plainly because “HIPAA compliant” is a claim that is frequently made and rarely true of consumer apps. Your protection here comes from the architecture in Sections 3 and 7 — which is, for this narrow purpose, a stronger guarantee than a regulatory one, because it does not depend on our good behaviour.

The FTC Health Breach Notification Rule does apply to us. See Section 14.

14. Security incidents and breach notification

We are subject to the FTC Health Breach Notification Rule, including its 2024 amendments, under which an unauthorised disclosure — not only an intrusion — is a reportable breach.

If we discover a breach of security involving personal data we hold, we will notify affected users and the Federal Trade Commission within the timeframes the Rule requires, and any other regulator entitled to notice under applicable state or foreign law. Where the law sets a 60-day individual notification clock, we will meet it.

The Rule’s notification duties attach to unsecured health information. Records encrypted on the user’s device under keys our servers never hold are a strong case for falling outside that definition, and our encryption standard is documented and dated for exactly that reason. We will not, however, use that position as a reason to stay silent: if our systems are breached, we will tell affected users what happened, whether or not a notification duty is triggered.

No system can guarantee absolute security. What we can guarantee is that an attacker who obtains our database obtains ciphertext, and that we have not kept a key anywhere for them to find.

To report a vulnerability, email apex@apextechnologygroup.io. We will not pursue legal action against good-faith security research that respects user privacy, avoids service degradation, and gives us reasonable time to remediate before disclosure.
